**Senior / Lead DevOps Engineer Interview Prep** — Nordics, EU, US, Australia

**Round 1: Kubernetes & Containers**

*Q1: Two teams on a shared cluster keep colliding over resources. Fix it, technically and as a lead.*
"My first instinct isn't a technical fix, it's to understand why two teams are colliding on shared infrastructure, because that usually points to a process gap. In the short term, I'd apply resource quotas and limit ranges at the namespace level for both teams, and set priority classes so production workloads can preempt batch jobs if there's contention. In parallel, I'd bring both teams together to understand their actual capacity needs, and propose a capacity-planning or chargeback model so usage is visible and fair. And if their workloads are fundamentally different in nature, I'd evaluate whether separate node pools or even separate clusters makes more sense long-term."

*Q2: A stateful database needs its node drained, zero downtime allowed.*
"First, a PodDisruptionBudget in place beforehand, so a drain can't take down more replicas than the budget allows. Second, the database runs as a StatefulSet with proper replication, so there's always a standby. Third, the drain cordons the node and respects that budget, gracefully rescheduling pods — and for something like Postgres with Patroni, you'd trigger a controlled failover to a replica first. As a lead, this whole process should be rehearsed and documented as a runbook."

*Q3: Cluster costs crept up forty percent this quarter with no traffic increase.*
"I'd break down cost by namespace and workload using something like Kubecost. Usually it's over-provisioned requests or zombie resources — old load balancers, unused volumes, forgotten dev environments. I'd push for VPA recommendations and autoscaler tuning, and as a lead, make cost visibility a recurring monthly review so it doesn't creep up silently again."

**Round 2: Cloud Infrastructure & IaC**

*Q1: Structuring Terraform across multiple teams at scale.*
"Reusable, versioned modules for networking, IAM, and compute, each environment with its own remote state and locking. At scale, a platform-team model works best — the platform team owns the modules and guardrails, application teams consume them through a self-service interface."

*Q2: Multi-cloud versus single-cloud.*
"I'm honestly skeptical of multi-cloud for its own sake, it multiplies complexity and rarely pays off. I'd only recommend it for genuine regulatory needs, avoiding critical vendor lock-in, or a best-in-class service unique to one provider."

*Q3: Secrets management as a lead.*
"A central secrets manager like Vault, never environment variables scattered across repos. Secrets injected at runtime, scoped tightly through IAM, with automatic rotation and audits, and a clear incident process for when one does leak."

**Round 3: CI/CD & GitOps**

*Q1: Designing a CI/CD pipeline for microservices.*
"Lint and unit tests on pull request, then a build stage producing a scanned, versioned image. Integration tests follow, then merging updates a GitOps repo like Argo CD, which syncs to staging automatically, and production is gated by approval or automated canary analysis through Argo Rollouts."

*Q2: Blue-green versus canary, and Argo Rollouts experience.*
"Blue-green gives instant rollback and a clean cutover but needs double infrastructure briefly. Canary catches issues with a fraction of real traffic first. I've used Argo Rollouts with Argo Workflows at IFS, configuring weighted canary steps checked against Prometheus-based analysis templates, with automatic rollback on breach."

*Q3: Rolling out a breaking API change to a dozen consuming services.*
"Never a single cutover — version the API, run old and new side-by-side, give teams a deprecation window with migration guides. Add contract testing like Pact into CI so breaking changes are caught before merge. As the lead, the real job is coordinating the timeline across teams."

**Round 4: Monitoring & Observability**

*Q1: Monitoring versus observability.*
"Monitoring tells you something's wrong based on thresholds you already thought to track. Observability lets you ask new questions you didn't anticipate, through metrics, logs, and traces together — critical as systems get more distributed and failure modes less predictable."

*Q2: Defining SLIs and SLOs, avoiding alert fatigue.*
"Start from the user's experience — latency or success rate as the client sees it. The SLO gives you an error budget, and alerts should fire on burn rate, not every blip. As a lead, regularly review which alerts actually get acted on and kill the rest."

*Q3: Leading incident response and postmortems.*
"A single incident commander coordinating, a running timeline captured live, fixed-cadence stakeholder updates. Postmortems are blameless by design, focused on systemic cause, and every one produces action items with owners and dates that I track to completion."

**Round 5: Leadership & Behavioral**

*Q1: A technical decision your team disagreed with.*
"I pushed for GitOps migration, two engineers preferred the old approach. Rather than overruling them, I ran a two-week parallel trial measuring rollback time, and the data won them over naturally. The lesson: a lead's job is creating conditions where the best answer becomes obvious."

*Q2: Mentoring engineers from mid-level to senior.*
"Less about more tools, more about shifting how they think about trade-offs — handing them ambiguous problems, asking for options not just solutions, and letting them own postmortems and design reviews."

*Q3: Balancing hands-on work with leadership.*
"I protect hands-on time deliberately for credibility, but selectively — the highest-risk pieces. The rest of my job is unblocking people and documenting direction so it doesn't all live in my head."
